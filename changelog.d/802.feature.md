## Voice control in the desktop app

The desktop app can now be driven by voice. Press **Voice**, say *"show me the overview"* or *"open the tester"* or *"go back"*, and the app does it — and says what it did. The button stays on and keeps listening: say the next command when you are ready, and press it again when you are done. The button itself says which it is, **Voice on** or **Voice off**, and it will not say *off* over a microphone it has been unable to close — while it is letting go it says **Voice stopping…**, and if it cannot let go it says so and offers you the retry rather than quietly claiming the device is free. That holds however the app got there, including a window that was reloaded or crashed out from under a recording and came back to find it still running.

**You do not press anything between commands.** The app works out where one utterance ends by listening for you to stop speaking — about eight tenths of a second of quiet — and then transcribes that much and nothing else. Speak again and the next one starts.

**Speaking needs no key, and your voice does not have to leave the machine.** Speech starts on a small speech container you run yourself on `127.0.0.1` — one `docker run`, no account, no credential — and Settings → Voice tells you the command. If it is not running, pressing Voice says exactly that and names the command to type rather than reporting a failure. Prefer a hosted service instead? Pick **OpenAI** in the same panel and paste your key; it goes to your operating system's keychain and never into a settings file.

**Turning a sentence into an action does need a key, and that is a deliberate choice rather than a gap.** An earlier build of this feature used the `claude` CLI you are already signed in to, so it asked you for nothing; that option is gone. Two reasons. Once a stage spends a credential at all, you should be the one choosing whose it is — we cannot assume everyone uses the provider we happened to pick — and a program we launch on your machine gives you nothing to choose. And we measured the credential-free alternative twice: a small model running locally got 20 of our 24 test phrases right, but one of the four it got wrong turned *"what time is it?"* into a command, which is exactly the mistake the *"none of these"* answer exists to prevent. So Settings → Voice asks for a key for this half; it goes to your operating system's keychain rather than into any settings file, and answers come back in about a second. You choose whose key it is — Anthropic, OpenAI, or anything that speaks either shape. Speech still needs no key at all. (If you point Commands at a server on **your own machine**, it asks for no key either, and sends none.)

**One key runs the whole thing.** Commands starts on **OpenAI**, which is also the hosted option for speech — so if you want voice hosted end to end, that is one account and one key pasted twice, rather than two companies to sign up with before you can press a button. Prefer Anthropic for the command half? Pick it in the same panel and paste that key instead; it is exactly as supported as it was, and a settings file that already names it keeps naming it. Want to pay for none of it? Leave speech on the container on your own machine — still the default — and only the command half asks for anything.

**Both halves name their provider and let you point them somewhere else.** Each option says whose key it wants, so you know which one to paste, and each one shows the **endpoint** and the **model** it will use. Commands offers two: **Anthropic API**, and **OpenAI-compatible API** for OpenAI itself or any of the many services and local servers that speak the same `/v1/chat/completions` shape. Change any of it and voice goes there instead — a different speech container, a compatible gateway, a cheaper or larger model — on the next thing you say, with no restart. Commands has one more setting, **Max tokens**, which is how much answer one command is allowed to cost. It matters because newer models *think* before they answer and the thinking counts against the same allowance: set it too low and answers come back cut off with nothing in them. It is a ceiling and not a reservation — it costs you nothing unless it is used — so the default is generous, and if you do point Commands at a model that thinks a lot, the message you get says the number and which setting to raise rather than just telling you something was cut off. The endpoint is checked when it is saved: it must be `https`, or `http` only to your own machine, and it may not carry a username or password. The keyless speech option has one extra rule, because it sends no key at all: its endpoint has to be **on your own machine**. Point it somewhere else and the app refuses the setting rather than quietly uploading what you said to a service you gave no credential to.

**What it can do today.** Move around — open one agent's pane, open the agent overview, go back to the deck, close an agent view, open settings. Stop listening: say *"voice off"*, *"turn off the voice"* or *"stop listening"* from any screen. Find out what it knows: say *"what can I say?"* and it shows you the list, generated from the commands it actually has rather than from a page somebody remembered to update. And **talk to one of your agents** — see below. Nothing that starts an agent, stops one, or answers a permission prompt is reachable by voice, and that is deliberate rather than unfinished — those are the commands where a rare misfire is expensive, and they are waiting on a confirmation step of their own. "Close the agent view" closes the *view*; it does not stop the agent and does not close its terminal pane.

**Open an agent's pane, say *"type run the login tests"*, and those words go into that agent's prompt instead of being treated as a command.** The words that introduced the sentence — *"type"* here — are stripped, and the rest is typed exactly as you said it, **where you can see it and edit it**, not into a hidden buffer somewhere. There is no magic word to remember: *"write a note that the build is green"*, *"tell it to run the tests"* and *"let's write a prompt…"* all work too. Whichever you use, **what arrives in the prompt is your own words, untouched** — the model is asked only where your sentence starts, never for the sentence itself, and if what comes back is not genuinely how you started, nothing is typed rather than something you did not say. Which agent gets it is simply the one whose pane is open: say it with nothing open and you are told to open a pane first, so your words cannot land in a terminal you are not looking at.

**Nothing is sent until you say so, and you can watch the send coming.** Say *"end"* — or *"send it"*, *"submit"*, *"enter"* — and it goes at once; press Enter yourself and it goes; or say nothing for about five seconds and it goes then, with the row counting those seconds down and naming the agent it is about to send to. **Talking stops that clock**, so a pause in the middle of a thought — *"can you check…"* — does not deliver half an instruction to an agent and leave you wondering what it got. Carry on and the rest is typed on the end and the count starts again; say anything else and what is already there simply stays in the prompt, yours to send or edit. The Voice button stops it too, which is the way out that still works when what you said was misheard. Nothing has been sent, so it is a line to fix rather than a message to unsend.

**Asking for something it cannot do gets you a useful answer rather than a shrug.** Ask for settings while you are on the agent overview and it tells you what it needs first, naming the prerequisite — *settings open from the deck*. Ask for something it has no command for and it says so and **shows you exactly what it heard**, so a mis-transcription is something you can correct instead of a mystery. Every sentence you see comes from the app, not from the model — the model picks an action from a fixed list and never writes prose you read. After a command that moved you somewhere, an **Undo** sits in the report for ten seconds.

**A cough, a keystroke or a chair creak is reported as nothing said, not as something you said.** A noise is enough to end one stretch of listening, and speech models handed a quiet room do not answer "nothing" — they answer with the stock phrases they were trained on, so an early build told people it had heard *"Don't forget to subscribe"*. The app now checks there was actually somebody speaking before it sends anything, and spends no transcription or model call on it. It is not a fault in your microphone and does not read like one.

**And it works that out from your own recording rather than from a fixed loudness, so a quiet microphone is not mistaken for a quiet room.** Microphones differ in level by more than voices do, so a single "this is loud enough to be speech" number is wrong for somebody whatever it is set to: high enough to keep a noisy room out and it refuses a quiet input; low enough for the quiet input and the noisy room gets through. It now compares what you said against the room *in the same recording* — speech has to stand out from your own background, which is a thing every recording carries with it — so the same voice gets the same answer on a laptop's built-in microphone and on a studio one, with nothing to set up and nothing to calibrate. **When it does refuse, it shows you its working**: what the loudest moment measured, what your room measured, and what it needed. If the two are far apart and you were still refused you said too little; if they are close together, the room is loud and moving the microphone will do more than speaking up.

**The button and what it heard live in a row of their own along the bottom, and nothing covers it.** The report used to float over the screen, including over an agent you had enlarged to full window — exactly the thing you were trying to read. It now has reserved space: the button on the left, what it heard and what it did on the right, and every other surface stops above it. The last report stays there until the next thing you say replaces it, so you can look away and look back. **Before you have said anything it tells you how to stop and how to see the list**, which is the one moment you are looking at it and have not yet been told either.

**Talk for thirty seconds without pausing and it throws that away rather than charging you for it.** A command here is one to four words, so half a minute of unbroken speech is a conversation in the room or a microphone pointed at a call — never a command. The app says so briefly and keeps listening, and no transcription or model call is spent on it.

**Your machine stays awake while voice is on, and goes back to normal the moment it is off.** Talking to a computer produces no mouse movement and no keystrokes, and that is all an idle timer counts — so the one state in which you are most obviously *using* the machine is the one it reads as you having walked away, and it goes to sleep in the middle of your session. Turn Voice on and the app asks the operating system to stay awake for as long as it is on; turn it off and it stops asking, immediately. There is nothing to configure and nothing new to remember: the Voice button you already press is the whole control, and it is also the indicator, so there is never a *keep awake* setting left on by accident. Two things it deliberately does not do. It does not keep your **screen** lit — you are talking, not watching, and that is battery you did not ask to spend — so the display still dims and blanks on its own schedule. And it does not survive the app: if this app is quit, crashes, or is killed outright, your machine goes straight back to sleeping normally, because the operating system itself takes the request back rather than trusting us to. On a machine or a session that will not grant the request at all — a locked-down work laptop, a remote box, a desktop whose power settings are managed elsewhere — voice control works exactly as it does everywhere else, and it simply does not keep that machine awake. You get no error for it, because there would be nothing for you to do about one.

**Nothing you say is logged or saved by this app.** No transcript, utterance or audio buffer is written to a file or a log anywhere, and the recording is turned into text before it ever leaves the Rust side of the app. Two limits on that, stated because the unqualified version would be misleading. Whenever you point a stage at a service — a hosted one, or the speech container on your own machine — your words go there, and what it keeps is that service's business and not something this app can promise anything about. And **dictation types what you said into an agent's prompt**, which is the whole point of it: from there your words are in that agent's session and go wherever that agent's own model and transcript go. You can see every word of it before it is sent, which is why it is typed somewhere visible rather than sent for you. (An earlier build had a second limit, because the default command backend handed your sentence to the `claude` CLI and could only *ask* that program not to keep a session. That backend is gone, so the app no longer hands what you said to any program running on your machine.)

**Known limits worth reading before you rely on it.** Dictation does not check beforehand that the agent whose pane is open can accept typing — an agent that has finished, or whose pane this app can only read, refuses the words as you say them, and the refusal is what comes back in the row rather than your sentence going anywhere. There is one activation mode — press on, press off, with the app finding the end of each utterance in between — and hold-to-talk is still to come. The microphone is closed while a command is being transcribed and worked out, so speech during those few seconds is not captured; the report says which of the two it is doing. The keyless speech option is a container **you** start and keep running; the app does not install, launch or manage it, and does not check that it is up until you speak. And the **microphone permission prompt has not been exercised on a packaged build on any platform**: the app carries the macOS usage description it needs, but no automated test in this project can open a real microphone or drive the real app window, so the first time you press that button is genuinely the first time. **Keeping the machine awake carries a narrower version of the same warning.** The request itself is made and checked on all three systems every time we change the code, and on Linux it has also been watched going in and coming back out, including after the app was killed outright. What nobody here has watched is the part that matters to you — a machine actually not falling asleep — so if yours still sleeps mid-sentence, that is worth reporting rather than assuming it is your settings. On Linux there is one known gap besides: a few desktops decide when to suspend entirely by themselves and never ask the system whether anything objects, and on those the request is granted and ignored.

The desktop app remains an unsigned alpha outside the CLI's support expectations. Maintainer notes — the pipeline, the voice-activity detection that segments it, the command table and its three consumers, what adding a command actually costs, how dictation works and what cancels its countdown, what the build-time guard does and does not see, and the manual checks that cover what no test does — are in `docs/develop/desktop-gui.md`, which is maintainer documentation and is not published to the docs site.
