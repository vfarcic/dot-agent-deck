## Two `shell_activity_monitor` tests stop asserting against an idle box's schedule

`shell_activity_monitor_reflects_a_real_detached_shell_command` and `shell_activity_monitor_stamps_the_owning_agent_across_a_session_rollover` failed together in 1 of 10 `cargo test-fast` runs on a 16-core box. Neither was a behavioural failure, and the timings say so precisely: the first failed at **3.076 s** against a flat `Duration::from_secs(3)` and the second at **5.089 s** against a flat `Duration::from_secs(5)`. Each had exhausted its own first deadline plus overhead — the signature of a ceiling running out, not of a wrong value being observed, which would have failed at whenever that value appeared rather than within milliseconds of the budget.

What those ceilings bound is a **boot**, not a behaviour. Both tests drive a real `/bin/sh`, type a command that starts a **`python3` interpreter**, which then forks, `setsid`s and `execv`s a `sleep` — and only then can the monitor's process-table sample see anything. That whole chain is exactly what issue #709 is about, and both ceilings were flat and unscaled, which is precisely the pre-#709 configuration #709 exists to remove.

All three waits in the pair are now `load_scaled`, so a fast box still fails fast and a loaded one still passes. The helper is a small `#[cfg(test)]` module, `src/test_budget.rs`, because `tests/common/mod.rs`'s version is not reachable from the lib target — the same wall `src/test_isolation.rs` already documents, and it carries the same reasoning: an **unmeasurable** load yields a factor of 1.0 rather than the 6x maximum, and the factor is clamped rather than unbounded. Three unit tests cover those branches, including the non-Linux one that is otherwise unreachable on Linux.

**What is and is not demonstrated, since the distinction decides how much to trust this.** The diagnosis rests on the two failure timings matching their two different deadlines, and on the fix being #709's own remedy for that signature. It is *not* backed by a reproduce-then-refute run: synthetic CPU load does not reproduce the failure — the flat ceilings survived 12 of 12 runs at load average 37.9 on 16 cores — so what the tier supplies is process and IO contention from 160 concurrently running test binaries rather than raw CPU pressure, and that was not reconstructed outside a tier run. The change is one-directional and cheap either way: it only ever widens a ceiling, never narrows one, and on an idle box the factor is exactly 1.0, measured as identical timings before and after (2.212 s against 2.262 s, 0.604 s against 0.611 s). Six consecutive green tier runs followed.
